How to read stock charts: a plain-English guide for beginners

A stock chart is a picture of what buyers and sellers did. You do not need maths or indicators to start. You need five ideas and a lot of practice on real charts.

1. Candles: one period, four prices

Most charts use candlesticks. Each candle is one period, such as a day. Its body runs from the open to the close, and thin lines (wicks) reach the high and the low. A body that closed above its open is usually shown green, one that closed lower red. Long bodies mean a decisive day; long wicks mean price was pushed away from its extreme.

2. Trend: which way is it going?

A rising trend makes higher highs and higher lows; a falling trend makes lower highs and lower lows; a sideways market does neither. Before anything else, name the trend. Trading with a clear trend is usually easier than guessing a turn.

3. Support and resistance: floors and ceilings

Support is a level where buyers have repeatedly stepped in. Resistance is a level where sellers have repeatedly appeared. The more times price has reacted at a level, the more traders notice it. When a level finally breaks, it often matters more because many people were watching.

4. Volume: how many people agree

Volume is the number of shares traded. A move on heavy volume has more participation behind it than the same move on thin volume. Fading volume during a pause, then a surge on the breakout, is a classic sign of a healthy move.

5. Timeframe: pick one and stay consistent

A daily candle covers a whole day; a weekly candle covers a week. Longer timeframes are calmer and the patterns on them are usually more reliable; shorter ones are noisier. Choose the one that matches how long you plan to hold, and say it out loud: "daily candles, 10 days ahead".

Put it together: a simple routine

  1. Name the trend (up, down, sideways).
  2. Mark the nearest floor and ceiling.
  3. Check volume: is it rising or fading?
  4. Look for a known pattern, and check its rules.
  5. Decide what would prove you wrong before you decide what you want.

No chart tells you the future. The goal is to make better-informed guesses and to notice what you got wrong. Read how often patterns really work for the honest odds.

Frequently asked questions

What should a beginner look at first on a stock chart?

Start with the daily candlestick chart and answer three questions: which way is the trend, where are the obvious floors and ceilings, and is volume rising or fading?

What does a candlestick show?

Each candle shows one period (for example one day): the open, high, low and close. A green or filled-up candle closed higher than it opened; a red one closed lower.

Which timeframe should I use?

Match it to how long you plan to hold. Daily candles suit swing trades lasting days to weeks. Shorter timeframes are noisier.

Do I need indicators?

No. Price and volume come first. Indicators are optional and are built from price, so learn the price first.
